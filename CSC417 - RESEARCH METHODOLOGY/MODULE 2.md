## Week 2: Research Problem, Process, and Hypothesis

### 1. Formulation of Research Problem

#### Definition

A **Research Problem** is a clear and definite statement about an area of concern, a condition to be improved, a difficulty to be eliminated, or a troubling question in theory or practice.

- It points to the need for meaningful understanding and deliberate investigation.
    
- **What it is NOT:** It does not state _how_ to do something, it is not a vague proposition, and it does not present a value question (a question of right vs. wrong based on morals rather than facts).
    

#### Purpose of a Problem Statement

1. **Orientation:** Introduces the importance of the topic and the significance of the study.
    
2. **Context:** Defines the parameters of what is to be investigated.
    
3. **Framework:** Provides the structure for reporting results and indicates what is necessary to conduct the study.
    

#### Characteristics of an Effective Research Problem

For a problem to be researchable, it must be:

- **Significant:** Reflects important issues or needs.
    
- **Evidence-based:** Based on factual evidence (non-hypothetical).
    
- **Manageable:** Relevant and feasible within resource/time constraints.
    
- **Testable:** Suggests a meaningful hypothesis that can be tested (avoids useless answers).
    

#### Steps in Formulating the Problem

1. Specify research objectives.
    
2. Review the context or environment.
    
3. Explore the nature of the problem.
    
4. Determine variable relationships.
    
5. Anticipate possible consequences of alternative approaches.
    

### 2. Major Steps in the Research Process

Research is a cyclical process that generally follows these 8 stages:

#### Step 1: Identifying the Problem

The first and most critical task. A well-identified problem guides the entire study.

- _Researchable problems_ allow for thorough verification and data analysis.
    
- _Non-research problems_ do not require these systematic processes.
    

#### Step 2: Reviewing Literature

An integral part of the process where the researcher studies existing work.

- **Benefits:** Avoids duplication, reveals what is already known, provides methodological insights, and clarifies relevant theories and concepts.
    

#### Step 3: Setting Questions, Objectives, and Hypotheses

- **Objectives:** Precise statements of what should be researched and the type of information to be collected.
    
- **Hypothesis:** An unproven proposition or "educated guess" that can be refuted or supported by empirical data.
    

#### Step 4: Choosing the Study Design

The "blueprint" or master plan for the study. Common designs include:

- Survey
    
- Experiment
    
- Secondary Data Study
    
- Observational Study
    

#### Step 5: Deciding on the Sample Design

- **Population:** The total collection of elements (universe) about which we wish to make an inference.
    
- **Sample:** A carefully selected part of the population that represents the whole.
    
- **Sample Design:** The method used to select the sample and the formulas for computing statistics.
    

#### Step 6: Collecting Data

The gathering of information based on the design (e.g., observation, large-scale survey).

- **Quantitative Data:** Often collected via structured interviews or self-administered questionnaires.
    
- **Other Methods:** Telephone interviewing, observation.
    

#### Step 7: Processing and Analyzing Data

- **Processing:** Editing (checking for consistency/omissions) and Coding (categorizing data).
    
- **Analysis:** Reducing data to manageable sizes, summarizing, finding patterns, and applying statistical techniques to test hypotheses.
    

#### Step 8: Writing the Report

- **Research Proposal:** A work plan or statement of intent produced _before_ the study.
    
- **Research Report:** A document establishing credibility _after_ the study. It includes: Executive Summary, Background, Literature Review, Methodology, Findings, Conclusion/Recommendations, and References.
    

### 3. Hypothesis

#### Definition

A **Hypothesis** is a tentative assumption or proposition set forth as an explanation for the occurrence of some specified group of phenomena. It translates research questions into predictions.

- _Example:_ "Students who receive counseling will show a greater increase in creativity than students not receiving counseling."
    

#### Characteristics of a Good Hypothesis

1. **Clear and Precise:** Ambiguity leads to unreliable inferences.
    
2. **Testable:** Must be capable of verification through observation/experimentation.
    
3. **Relational:** Should state the relationship between variables.
    
4. **Specific:** Narrower hypotheses are generally more testable.
    
5. **Simple:** stated in the simplest terms possible (Parsimony).
    
6. **Consistent:** Aligns with a substantial body of established facts.
    
7. **Time-bound:** Amenable to testing within a reasonable timeframe.
    
8. **Empirical:** Must actually explain what it claims to explain based on facts.
    

#### Sources of Hypothesis

- Resemblance between phenomena.
    
- Observations from past studies or present-day experiences.
    
- Scientific theories.
    
- General patterns in thinking.
